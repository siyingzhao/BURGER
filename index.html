Abstract

How effectively can vision-language-action (VLA) models learn diverse behaviors as training data scales? BURGER makes this question accessible through language-conditioned robotic drawing. A scalable, prompt-driven pipeline converts generated sketches into continuous robot trajectories in simulation and on hardware. Starting from a blank canvas, a policy draws the object or spatial composition specified by language; each instruction allows many valid drawings and stroke sequences. We construct 28,200 simulated demonstrations across 282 tasks and evaluate five VLA models on data scaling, language grounding, spatial composition, and multimodal action generation. The results reveal distinct failure patterns, with some models benefiting from larger and more diverse datasets and generalizing to unseen combinations of familiar objects. Real-robot experiments with a fine-tuned π0.5 policy further demonstrate drawing with visual feedback, including sketch completion and responses to human-added strokes.

Motivation

A blank canvas makes language essential. Many valid drawings make behavior diversity measurable.

Studying how VLA models use larger and more diverse datasets requires control over the training data and a way to distinguish what each model learns. Robotic drawing provides three useful properties:

  1. Language specifies the goal. Every episode starts from the same blank canvas, so the initial image alone cannot reveal what to draw. The policy receives no target sketch at inference.
  2. Many right answers. One instruction admits many drawings that differ in shape, stroke order, start point and direction. Averaging incompatible trajectories can produce an unrecognizable drawing.
  3. Failures you can see. The canvas is the record: semantic, spatial and execution errors stay visible on paper.

We quantify trajectory diversity by comparing BURGER with LIBERO under the same clustering procedure.

How routes are counted

Planar end-effector paths, count-matched to 40 instructions × ≈42 episodes per dataset (all four LIBERO suites). Each path is resampled to 32 points and described by its start-relative xy path plus step vectors. One shared k-means codebook (k = 24) is fitted on the pooled trajectories of both datasets, and Neff = exp(−Σj pj log pj), where pj is the share of the instruction's trajectories in cluster j. The two distributions overlap at a single pair: LIBERO's most varied instruction (open the middle drawer of the cabinet, 1.96) against BURGER's least (draw a smiley face, 1.61).

Method

From a text prompt to recorded robot demonstrations, with control over task diversity and data scale.

2.1 From a prompt to a robot trajectory

Demonstrations are generated, not teleoperated. A text-to-image model draws a line-art sketch; the sketch is skeletonized into one-pixel strokes, which are ordered (top to bottom, outline before details, nearest stroke next); an end-effector pose controller replays the strokes with pen lifts between them while every observation and action is recorded. Choose an object to follow one generated sample through each stage.

2.3 The BURGER dataset

282 tasks × 100 demonstrations = 28,200 trajectories. 132 single objects, chosen from the Quick, Draw! categories as everyday things an arm can draw, and 150 compositions pairing base objects with a spatial relation (100 left of, 50 over / under). Stored as HDF5 and converted to standard robot-learning formats.

2.4 Evaluating drawings against human judgments

CLIP-style scores measure global image–text alignment and can miss fine errors, such as two objects on the wrong sides. We train a lightweight projection head on a frozen DINOv3 backbone so that drawings of a class cluster together, and score a drawing by its similarity to the target-class prototype. Compositions are split at the minimum-connectivity boundary, so each object and the relation are checked separately. In a user study, 66 participants ranked five drawings for each of 10 classes.

Experiments

How do models respond to more data, new object combinations, and multiple valid ways to draw?

We fine-tune π0, π0.5 and π0-FAST (flow-matching and FAST-tokenised policies from the OpenPI family), OpenVLA (autoregressive) and OpenVLA-OFT (parallel-decoded action chunks) on BURGER, and score every final canvas with the metric of Section 2.4.

3.1 Scaling benefits depend on the model

Training sets vary from 10 to 132 object classes and from 10 to 100 demonstrations per class. For π0 and π0.5, increasing either generally raises the drawing score. π0-FAST fluctuates more and does not consistently benefit from more data in these experiments.

3.2 Spatial relations transfer to unseen object pairs

The evaluated π0 and π0.5 policies can draw familiar objects in combinations absent from training, responding to relations such as left of, over, and under. This tests new combinations of known objects. Drawing quality can still degrade under composition, particularly with vertical relations, even when the relative placement is correct.

3.3 A circle separates execution quality from behavior diversity

A controlled task isolates action generation: 100 demonstrations share a circle's shape and radius while varying its center, starting point, and direction (56% clockwise). Each model setting is evaluated on 20 rollouts. Longer action chunks improve execution: OpenVLA-OFT makes contact in 6 of 20 rollouts with chunk length 1, compared with 20 of 20 at length 8. Behavior coverage remains uneven: π0-FAST draws clockwise in 19 of 20 rollouts, while π0.5 draws clockwise in 11 of 20 and retains greater starting-point diversity. OpenVLA-OFT and π0-FAST also continue beyond a single revolution.

3.4 Drawings reveal distinct failure patterns

OpenVLA and π0-FAST are sensitive to the initial end-effector pose: OpenVLA can get trapped in a local region, while π0-FAST can drift away from the target area. Flow-matching policies sometimes continue drawing after completing the object. The resulting canvases help distinguish local stagnation, drift, and overdraw—failures that a single success rate does not explain.

Successful rollouts can also retain multiple drawing behaviors. Across four representative tasks, π0.5 retains 3.6–5.1 effective trajectory modes per instruction, compared with approximately 7.5 in the training data.

Limitations

  • Simplified drawing physics. BURGER uses a rigid drawing tool to produce line sketches. Its scope does not include brush deformation or the dynamics of other drawing media.
  • Inherited biases. Sketches come from a text-to-image model and stroke order from a heuristic, so the data may encode drawing styles that differ from human drawing behavior.
  • Limited generalization to new objects. The evaluated policies can compose familiar objects in new arrangements, but tend to produce generic, unstructured strokes for object names absent from training. The observed compositional generalization does not establish generalization to unseen object categories.
